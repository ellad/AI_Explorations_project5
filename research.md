### Question 1: What does each ingredient do?

Sources: ISU (extension), PopSci (journalism), SE (Serious Eats, J. Kenji López-Alt, text supplied by Developer; the author reports 100+ tests and 1,536 cookies, which is his own account and not peer-reviewed). The three agree on direction. Only SE gives some numbers (see the ratio table below).

| Variable | Source says | Sources |
|---|---|---|
| Flour (protein) | Gluten gives structure, height and chew. All-bread-flour cookies are "ultra-chewy", all-cake-flour cookies "very soft, almost mushy". Sugar and fat restrict gluten for tenderness. | ISU, PopSci, SE (Fact 11) |
| Flour amount | Less flour relative to butter gives lacier, wafer-like cookies. More flour gives cookies that barely spread and stay dense and doughy. | SE (Fact 12) |
| Mixing | Overworking the flour builds gluten: smoother, tougher cookies. Barely mixing gives craggier cookies. | SE (Fact 13) |
| Butter amount | More butter gives a more tender cookie that spreads more (butter inhibits gluten). | SE (Fact 1), PopSci |
| Butter state | Creamed butter traps air (lighter, firmer). Melted butter gives squatter, denser, chewier cookies. Browned butter has no water, so less gluten and less dissolved sugar: softer, more tender, less caramel flavor. Warmer butter gives denser cookies. | ISU, PopSci, SE (Facts 2, 4, 7) |
| Butter substitutes | Shortening-based cookies come out softer but denser. Margarine or shortening gave "sub-par" flavor. | SE (Fact 1, 3), ISU |
| White sugar | Thin, crisp, wide-spreading cookies. It adds no acid, so soda has nothing to react with and gives no extra lift. It gives up moisture more readily. | ISU, SE (Fact 8) |
| Brown sugar | Tall, moist cookies. Slightly acidic, so it reacts with baking soda to give more rise, which limits spread (cakier). Contains glucose and fructose, which hold water more than sucrose. | ISU, SE (Fact 8) |
| Corn syrup | Replacing about 10% of the white sugar with corn syrup gave wide, flat, soft, flexible, darker cookies. | SE (Fact 9) |
| Dissolved vs. granular sugar | Fully dissolved sugar gave uniform texture and a smooth top. A mix of dissolved and granular gave craggier cookies. | SE (Fact 6, 8) |
| Baking soda | Needs an acid (such as brown sugar). Gives craggier, denser cookies. Alkaline conditions speed browning and the Maillard reaction. | ISU, PopSci, SE (Fact 10) |
| Baking powder | Contains its own acid. Cakier cookies that rise higher, with smoother, shinier tops. | ISU, SE (Fact 10) |
| Eggs | Proteins set with heat and shape texture. Extra whites (more water and protein) give taller cookies. Extra yolks (fat) give fudgier, brownie-like cookies. SE found 1:1 white to yolk best (natural egg). | ISU, PopSci, SE (Fact 5) |
| Salt | Balances sweetness and caramelized sugar flavor. SE says cookies "need more salt than you think". | PopSci, SE (Fact 16) |
| Dough temperature | Cold dough spreads less (more compact). Warm dough spreads more. | SE (Fact 19), PopSci, ISU |
| Resting / chilling | Rest for flavor: overnight or longer gives darker, more flavorful cookies. ISU: 24 h slows expansion and browns more evenly. PopSci: about 30 min lets flour hydrate and slows spread. | SE (Fact 20), ISU, PopSci |
| Oven temperature | Cooler oven gives wider, flatter, more evenly baked cookies. Hotter oven gives compact cookies. About 50°F makes a noticeable difference. | SE (Fact 18) |

**Numbers stated in SE (the only source read that gives any):**

| Quantity | Value | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Flour : sugar : butter | 1 : 1 : 0.8 by weight | "moderate spread", not cakey |
| Flour : butter | ≤ 1 : 1 → lacy, wafer-like. ≥ 1.3 : 1 → barely spreads, "dense and dough-like" (SE's words; "doughy" is the Agent's shorthand, and the threshold is from SE's own recipes, see reference recipes below). SE's choice: 10 oz : 8 oz (1.25 : 1) | Fact 12 |
| Corn syrup | 10% replacement of granulated sugar changed texture "completely" | Fact 9, one test |
| Baking soda | SE's choice: ¾ tsp in a recipe with 280 g flour | Fact 10 |
| Oven | 325°F in SE's recipe; tests ran 250–450°F | Fact 18 |

Caveats: these are one author's test results and preferences, using his own recipe (browned butter, hand-chopped chocolate), so they are a starting point for supported ranges, not proof. Two SE statements are explicitly the author's preference (cakey cookies are "not for me", moderate spread).

**Conflicts to resolve before coding rules:**
- Brown sugar and spread: **Resolved by SE.** Brown sugar causes more rise through its acidity and so *limits* spread. White sugar spreads wide. ISU agrees. The secondary summaries that said brown sugar increases spread are not supported by a source read directly, so the model should not use that claim.
- Chilling duration: 30 min (PopSci), 24 h (ISU), overnight to 3 days (SE) are different claims for different effects (spread vs. flavor). The model should only predict spread from dough temperature and not model rest time for flavor.
- Baking soda's effect on spread: ISU says it raises pH, which can increase spread. SE does not quantify it (only the ¾ tsp choice). Open.
- Toll House vs SE: the control uses 1 tsp baking soda with 294 g flour and 226 g butter. SE uses ¾ tsp (3 g) with 280 g flour and 225 g butter, so the control has about a third more soda per gram of flour. Whether that matters for the model is open.

### Question 3: Texture slider (dense and chewy ↔ thin and crispy)

- **Decision (Developer, 2026-11-02):** The slider runs from **dense and chewy** to **thin and crispy**. The pitch says "cakier or crispier" in three places (concept, experience goals, MVP), but the Developer meant chewy. The pitch text was corrected on 2026-11-02 (see Scope changes). This also resolves the earlier gap about where chewy fits: it is one end of the slider, and thickness is part of the same axis.
- **Why this works with the sources:** Sources describe two linked changes. Spread (wide and thin vs. compact and thick) and moisture (crisp vs. chewy). Granulated sugar, less flour and more butter give wide, thin, crisp cookies. Brown sugar, melted butter and more flour give compact, moist, dense cookies. "Cakey" (baking powder, extra whites, creamed butter) means tall and open-crumbed, which is a third direction and is *not* either end of this slider.
- **Source says, toward dense and chewy:**
  - Brown sugar: tall and moist, rises more through its acid reaction with the soda, which limits spread, and holds water (SE Fact 8, ISU). Kansas State via ISU: darker sugars make cookies chewier.
  - Melted butter: "squatter, denser" and "chewier" (SE Fact 2, ISU). Vegetable oil: "denser and flatter" (ISU).
  - More flour relative to butter: barely spreads, "barely spreads" with a "dense and dough-like" center at ≥ 1.3 : 1 (SE Fact 12; a reference point from SE's own recipes, not a limit).
  - Egg yolks: denser, fudgier, brownie-like (SE Fact 5).
- **Source says, toward thin and crispy:**
  - Granulated sugar: thin, crisp, wide-spreading, gives up moisture more readily (SE Fact 8, ISU).
  - Less flour relative to butter: lacy, wafer-like at ≤ 1 : 1 (SE Fact 12).
  - More butter: more spread and tenderness (SE Fact 1). Cooler oven gives wider, flatter cookies (SE Fact 18), though oven temperature is not an MVP variable.
- **Variables that break the single axis:**
  - **Corn syrup:** SE says it gives "wide, flat" cookies that are soft and chewy. That is thin *and* chewy, so it moves chewiness one way and thickness the other. The slider cannot move it without contradicting itself, so keep it as a separate substitution, not part of the slider group.
  - **Vegetable oil:** ISU says denser *and* flatter. Same issue, so also a separate substitution.
  - **Baking powder, extra egg white, creamed butter:** move toward cakey, so they stay as independent controls.
- **Resolved conflict:** SE called brown sugar "cakier" and ISU/Kansas State called it chewier. Both fit: brown sugar adds rise and holds moisture, so it moves toward the dense, chewy end and away from the thin, crispy end.
- **Decision (Developer, 2026-11-02): Slider group.** The slider moves the sugar split (more brown ↔ more white), flour : butter (higher ↔ lower), and egg yolk share (more yolk ↔ less yolk) together. Each has a sourced direction. No source gives amounts, so the size of each shift is a design choice and must be labeled as one.
- **Decision (Developer, 2026-11-02): Control position.** The Toll House control sits at the **middle** of the slider.
- **Model implication:** The model should still report separate qualities (spread, thickness, moisture, browning, crispness, chewiness) because the corn syrup and oil cases show they can move independently. The slider is a shortcut that moves several of them together.
- **Open:**
  1. Which variables the slider moves, and how far. The flour : butter range runs from lacy to doughy, so the slider's limits need a bound inside it.
  2. Whether the middle position holds up against test bakes (the control may not actually sit in the middle of the real texture range).
  3. Egg yolk share: the control has a natural 1 : 1 white to yolk, and the slider moving it needs bounds. SE found 1 : 1 best, so the slider may move it only a little.

### Question 4: Supported substitutions

- **Source says (ISU):** Margarine may spread more. Shortening gives higher rise and keeps shape. Vegetable oil gives denser, flatter cookies.
- **Source says (SE):** Bread flour for chew, cake flour for softness. Baking powder for cakier. Corn syrup for softer, wider. Two whites instead of a whole egg's yolk for taller. Shortening for softer, denser.
- **Open:** Honey or other sweeteners are not sourced. Amounts for the three MVP substitutions are in the next section.

#### Butter → vegetable oil

- **Source says (ISU):** Vegetable oil gives cookies that are "denser and flatter."
- **Source says (SE):** Butter is about 80–83% butterfat, 15% water and 3–5% milk protein. Water in butter helps gluten form. Browned butter, which has lost its water, gave softer, more tender cookies and left sugar undissolved. Butter's milk proteins add browning and flavor, and substituting less flavorful fats gave "sub-par" cookies.
- **Source says (UBC student study, [Chen et al., *The Expedition* 13, 2022](https://ojs.library.ubc.ca/index.php/expedition/article/view/197326)):** In sugar cookies, spread differed significantly between butter and coconut oil, avocado oil and peanut butter, but not between butter and lard. The Agent read only the abstract, so there are no numbers, and it is an undergraduate journal.
- **Unverified (blog and search-summary claims, not read directly):** Oil gives more spread and chewier cookies. A common conversion is about ¾ the butter amount, because butter is about 80% fat. The conversion is consistent with SE's butterfat figure.
- **Assumption (Agent):** Oil adds no water or milk solids, so by analogy with SE's browned-butter result it should mean less gluten, less browning flavor and a softer, flatter cookie. No source read tests this in chocolate chip cookies.
- **Gap:** No controlled measurement of oil vs. butter spread in cookies. Predictions should carry a low-confidence label.

#### Amounts for the three MVP substitutions (researched 2026-11-02)

Search results were mostly low-quality recipe and SEO sites. Only pages the Agent read directly are cited. KA = King Arthur Baking, ATK = America's Test Kitchen.

**Butter → vegetable oil**
- **Source says (ATK, ["Chewy, Cakey, or Thin and Crispy"](https://www.americastestkitchen.com/lessons/articles/2363-chewy-cakey-or-thin-and-crispy-how-to-manipulate-texture), no author or date on page):** For chewy cookies, aim for about **3 : 1 unsaturated (oil) to saturated (butter) fat**. Melted butter also helps develop gluten for chew. This is the only amount for oil in cookies found on a reputable page, and it is a ratio of fats, not a weight conversion.
- **Source says (Milk Street, [article](https://www.177milkstreet.com/2019/04/when-can-you-substitute-oil-for-butter), April 2019, no author):** Butter is about 16% water and oil has none, so scale oil back "to prevent greasiness". Oil "cannot trap and hold air the way softened butter can," so it is not suitable for creamed recipes. Melted-butter cookies have "a bit more wiggle room." No conversion number.
- **Source says (KA, ["Cookie chemistry"](https://www.kingarthurbaking.com/blog/2015/07/09/cookie-chemistry), PJ Hamel, 2015):** In a sugar cookie test with equal-volume swaps, oil-based cookies were tender and crumbly but greasy, and "tasted like old cooking oil". Butter was best for flavor.
- **Source says (KA oil search summary, cakes, not read in full):** Swapping about 25% of the butter by volume for oil keeps cakes moist. Cakes, not cookies.
- **Derived (Agent):** SE gives butter as 80–83% fat. Replacing butter with oil by fat content gives about 0.8 × the butter weight (about 181 g of oil for the control's 226 g). Blogs say ¾ (about 170 g). Both come from the water content argument, but no reputable page states a cookie conversion.
- **Implication for the model:** The control creams butter, and Milk Street says oil cannot replace creamed butter's air. ATK's 3 : 1 ratio (75% oil) is the only evidence for a large oil share. A **partial swap is better supported than a full one**. The supported range should probably stop below 100% oil, and a full swap should trigger the out-of-range warning.

**Corn syrup for some of the sugar**
- **Source says (SE Fact 9):** Replacing about 10% of the sugar (by weight: 1 oz of 10 oz total sugar, taken from the granulated sugar) changes texture completely. Wide, flat, soft and flexible cookies, darker color.
- **Source says (KA, ["Corn syrup in recipes"](https://www.kingarthurbaking.com/blog/2018/09/24/corn-syrup-question), Susan Reid, Sept 24, 2018):** An invert sugar that gives a "bendy and chewy" texture, and "prevent[s] grains of sugar from recrystallizing when the cookie cools, making it less brittle." Overbaking still gives a crisp cookie. For a crisp edge and bendy center, add about **1 tablespoon** of invert sugar to a recipe. It gives no replacement ratio and does not mention spread.
- **Source says (ATK, as above):** Liquid sweeteners such as corn syrup or molasses hold moisture more than brown sugar. Too much makes a cookie "too moist" (cakey end).
- **Derived (Agent):** SE's 10% of the control's 315 g of sugar is about 31 g. KA's 1 tbsp is a smaller, separate figure. Both are small shifts. Corn syrup's weight per tablespoon is not sourced.
- **Gap:** No source gives a ceiling. Corn syrup also moves spread (SE: wider), so the model needs a cap.

**Baking powder for some of the baking soda**
- **Source says (KA, ["What's the difference between baking soda and baking powder?"](https://www.kingarthurbaking.com/blog/2021/09/10/baking-soda-vs-baking-powder-substitutions), Justine Lee, Sept 10, 2021; also [Bob's Red Mill](https://www.bobsredmill.com/articles/making-cookies-without-baking-soda), Aug 8, 2018):** A full swap uses **3 times** as much baking powder as soda by volume (3 tsp powder for 1 tsp soda). - **Conflict:** Bob's Red Mill says soda gives a "coarse, chewy" texture and powder a "light, fine" one. ATK says a soda-and-powder mix is chewiest. SE says soda is denser and craggier. All agree powder moves toward cakey, so a small amount of powder is the supported direction.

**Amounts evidence for the slider and ranges (KA, ["Cookie science"](https://www.kingarthurbaking.com/blog/2016/12/21/cookie-science), Gwen Adams, Dec 21, 2016):**

#### Egg → applesauce and other egg substitutes (cut from the MVP; kept as research for later)

- **Role of eggs (SE):** Other than the small amount in butter, eggs are the main water source in the dough. Whites supply water and protein and give rise and height. Yolks supply fat and tenderness. ISU: egg proteins set and shape the cookie. Iowa State's egg article says eggs in cookies are "mainly for binding."
- **Applesauce:** ¼ cup replaces 1 egg ([Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/live-well-eat-well/2023-02-10-egg-substitutes-when-baking), 2023, and [University of Maine Extension](https://extension.umaine.edu/food-health/2023/02/16/using-egg-substitutes-in-baking-and-cooking/), 2023). Illinois classes it as a **moisture** replacement, not a leavening one. Maine says it "doesn't provide as much leavening as other options," "affects flavor and texture," and is best in quickbreads. Maine tested substitutes in a yellow cake, not cookies.
- **Flax egg:** 1 tbsp ground flax + 3 tbsp water replaces 1 egg as a **binder** (Illinois, Maine, [Iowa State](https://spendsmart.extension.iastate.edu/spendsmart/2025/03/10/baking-without-eggs-part-2/), 2025). Illinois adds ½ tsp baking powder when lift is needed, as in cookies. Iowa State tested flax eggs in oatmeal bars (not chocolate chip cookies) and said they turned out well.
- **Other (single extension sources):** ¼ cup commercial egg substitute or 2 egg whites per egg (Illinois), ¼ cup seltzer per egg for best texture in a yellow cake (Maine).
- **Unverified (blog tests found by search, not read directly):** Applesauce cookies are softer, puffier and more cake-like, "like a muffin top". Flax cookies stay soft longer. A commercial replacer matched the egg cookie but was slightly gray. Treat as anecdotes.
- **Gap:** No source tests applesauce, or any egg substitute, in a chocolate chip cookie with measurements. The extension sources describe the egg's function (moisture, binding, leavening) and which substitute covers which function. A prediction can follow from that mapping: applesauce replaces water but not structure or rise, so the likely effect is a softer, less set, cakier-or-denser cookie. This is an inference from function, not a tested result, and should carry a low-confidence label.
- **Decision (Developer, 2026-11-02):** Egg substitution is cut from the MVP. Butter → oil is the only substitution besides the corn syrup and baking powder swaps. Egg substitutes can return as a stretch goal if the model is stable.

**Mechanism for crisp vs. chewy (the slider)**
- Crisp or soft is a **water and sugar-state** question. Granulated sugar recrystallizes as the cookie cools, giving a crisp texture. Invert sugars (brown sugar, corn syrup, honey) interfere with recrystallization and hold water, so cookies stay bendy and chewy (KA, ATK; McGee excerpts).
- Lower water activity gives crisp textures and higher gives soft, chewy ones (vendor and food-science summaries, tier C).
- **Bake time confounds texture.** KA: overbaking gives a crisp cookie even with invert sugar. A patent (Tier C) adds that cookies lose their crisp-chewy contrast with storage as moisture and sugar equalize.

**What this changes**
1. **Oil's effect on spread is not settled.** Sources split. Texture claims (denser, chewier, tender, greasy at high amounts) are more consistent.
2. **Egg ratio's effect on spread is weak or conflicting.** Texture (fudgier, chewier) has two B-tier sources.
3. **Baking soda amount is uncertain in both directions** (more height and less spread vs. higher pH and more spread). Keeping it fixed at 6 g is supported.
4. **Brown sugar → less spread** has SE, ISU and one student study. One 2019 study disagrees. Unread, so a medium-confidence direction.
5. **Chilling is the best-evidenced variable not in the MVP** (three independent sources, two measured). The model assumes dough is baked right after mixing, and the site should say so.

### Egg amount and baking soda on the slider: evidence

**Egg amount**
- **Recipes:** The control has 0.34 g egg per g flour (2 eggs). Thin, crisp, or bendy-center recipes use fewer eggs for their flour: Tate's 0.22, KA Classic 0.21, KA Joy and ATK 0.25 and 0.23 (1 egg + 1 yolk), KA Easy 0.28. SE and Levain match the control (0.36, 0.35).

A chef test (±40% egg) found no clear spread change but a taller, cakier cookie with more egg. A Dickinson College class test says doubling the egg in a sugar cookie made it puff, and 1 tbsp of water restored spread (tier C, not read in full).
- **Likely effect of lowering egg toward the crispy end:** thinner, crisper, less lift. **Likely effect of raising it:** taller, cakier, which is away from both slider ends. So egg should only move toward the crispy end and stay at 2 eggs from the middle to the dense end.
- **Practical limit:** Eggs come in whole units. Real recipes use 1 egg (50 g), 1 egg + 1 yolk (about 68 g) or 2 eggs (100 g). A slider that gives 75 g of egg means weighing beaten egg, which is awkward for beginners. Using 1 egg at the crispy end (as Tate's does) and 2 eggs otherwise is feasible. A 1 egg + 1 yolk step would change the white : yolk mix, which the Developer ruled out except as a special case.
- **Confidence:** Medium for direction (ATK, SE, the Tate's and KA recipes), low for amounts.

**Baking soda**
- **Recipes (teaspoons per 100 g flour):** ATK 0.17, KA Easy 0.14 (plus powder), SE 0.27, control 0.34, Levain 0.35 (plus powder), Joy 0.37, KA Classic 0.42 (plus vinegar), **Tate's 0.67**. Tate's higher soda is proportional to its higher brown sugar. SE and KA explain that soda reacts with brown sugar's acid, so soda probably should move with the brown sugar, not on its own.
- **Sources on effect:** ATK says extra soda "helps dry out a cookie, giving it crispness." SE says soda browns faster (alkaline conditions speed Maillard) and gives craggy cookies. ISU says soda raises pH and can increase spread, and an excess leaves an alkaline flavor. A trade article says more bicarbonate gives more height and less spread. A chef test found leavening had the smallest effect. Search results link too much soda to a soapy or metallic taste when the acid is used up. No source gives a safe upper limit.

### Browning (Maillard reaction): evidence (2026-11-02; browning struck from the MVP)

The Developer asked for sources on the Maillard reaction, time and temperature, and said to strike browning if nothing supports it.

**What the sources say**
- **Mechanism (SE, text supplied by Developer):** Proteins in flour and eggs brown along with the sugar by the Maillard reaction. Sugar granules at the edges and underside melt and then caramelize. SE says the Maillard reaction "takes place more readily in mildly alkaline environments," so baking soda "has a powerful effect on how rapidly foods darken and develop browned flavors." Butter's milk proteins brown and add nutty, butterscotch notes. Brown sugar "caramelizes more readily" than granulated, and corn syrup, made of simple sugars, "caramelizes more readily, leading to darker overall color."
- **Baking soda test (Dickinson College class blog, ["The Science Behind Baking Soda"](https://blogs.dickinson.edu/chemistryinthekitchen/2020/05/01/the-science-behind-baking-soda/), 2020, tier C, read in full):** In a sugar cookie, doubling the soda (¼ → ½ tsp) gave a noticeably darker, flatter, crispy and firm cookie with a soapy taste. Halving it gave a very pale, bulky, undercooked center. This single test fits SE: more soda, more browning, crisper, and a flavor risk when the acid runs out.
- **Time and temperature:** SE Fact 18 says a cooler oven spreads cookies wider, bakes more evenly and loses the edge-to-center contrast below about 275°F. KA says shorter baking gives softer cookies and longer baking crunchier ones, and overbaking gives a crisp cookie even with invert sugar. Nestlé's recipe uses a color cue: "until golden brown" (9–11 min at 375°F). Search summaries of peer-reviewed work (kinetics in model cookies, a flour-products review, a Czech colour study on bakery products) agree that higher temperature and longer time give more browning, and that browning needs a hot, dry surface (the review gives above about 105–120°C at water activity 0.4–0.7). The Agent did not read those papers in full, and none gives color-versus-time numbers for chocolate chip cookies.
- **Reducing sugars:** A patent (tier C) says fructose and glucose are reducing sugars that brown readily at pH above 6. That supports brown sugar (which contains glucose and fructose, per SE) and corn syrup.

**What is missing:** No measured colour data (L*, a*, b*) for chocolate chip cookies, and no amounts. Some studies found colour unchanged across small time or temperature differences, so magnitude is not predictable from what I found.

**Reading (Agent):** Browning is **supported as a direction** by SE (a named test kitchen), the Dickinson test and the mechanism sources. It is **not supported for amounts**. Drivers the model controls: brown sugar share (up → darker), corn syrup (darker), baking soda (up → darker, linked to brown sugar), oil replacing butter (less milk protein → likely paler, an inference from SE's butter statement), thinner cookies (edges brown first, SE).
