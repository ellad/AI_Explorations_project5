## Ideation

### Ideas

#### Interactive baking experimenter

My first idea was a website that helps people learn more about baking and the food science behind it. Users could start with a base recipe, explore possible add-ins, swap one ingredient for another, or adjust ingredient ratios. The site would then show how those changes would likely affect the final bake. I also thought about including technique changes, such as using melted instead of softened butter or chilling the dough before baking.

This idea interests me because I already enjoy baking, and I am part of the audience I would be designing for. The biggest concern is that ingredients can interact in complicated ways, so it would not be realistic to support every recipe or promise an exact result in five weeks. I would need to start with one type of baked good and clearly present the results as researched predictions, not guarantees.

#### Scam-awareness assistant

My other idea was a website that helps older adults identify possible online scams. A user could upload a screenshot of a suspicious email or text and receive information about how likely it is to be a scam, what type of attack it might be, what warning signs to look for, and how to report it.

I liked this idea because I wanted to make something that could help a vulnerable population. However, it would be difficult to make a trustworthy version in five weeks. A wrong answer could have serious consequences, especially if the site incorrectly told someone that a message was safe. Screenshots could also contain private information. Making the tool accurate, protecting user privacy, and testing it with older adults would require more time than this project allows.

### Choice

I chose the baking website because it connects to one of my own interests and feels more achievable within five weeks. It gives me room to explore food science, interactive design, and visual communication without the same privacy and safety risks as the scam idea. I can keep the first version focused on chocolate-chip cookies while leaving room to add other baked goods later.

I decided not to continue with the scam-awareness idea because I did not think I could make it genuinely useful and responsible within the project timeline.

### Branding

I considered names such as *Crumb Theory*, *Batch Theory*, *The Cookie Lab*, and *Half Baked*. I chose **Half Baked** with the tagline **Experiment Before You Bake**. The name is memorable and a little playful, but it can still work if the website eventually expands beyond cookies.

One logo idea is a baked item divided in half, with one side finished and the other side made from measurement marks or ingredient diagrams. Another option is a wordmark that includes something like an oven dial or measuring line. I want the site to feel somewhere between a warm home kitchen and a food-science tool. I am considering warm brown and cream colors with a muted blue or teal accent. The design should feel approachable without becoming too childish or too clinical.

## Pitch

Keep this section to about 1,000 words.

### Concept

**Half Baked** is an interactive website that helps home bakers understand the food science behind a recipe. A base chocolate-chip cookie recipe will load automatically, and users can move a texture control toward a cakier or crispier cookie to see which parts of the formula change. They can then experiment with individual ingredient substitutions and ratios before trying the changes themselves. The site will show how a change is likely to affect qualities such as spread, thickness, moisture, browning, crispness, and chewiness. It will also explain why those effects are expected instead of only showing a result.

The website will not claim that it can perfectly simulate baking. Ingredients and techniques can affect each other in complicated ways, so the site will only make predictions for changes that I have researched and that fall within a supported range.

### Title and branding

The working title is **Half Baked**, and the tagline is **Experiment Before You Bake**. I want the logo to combine a baked item with measurement or diagram elements. Warm baking-inspired colors, a cooler accent color, measurement marks, and readable type will make the site feel polished and organized, but still friendly and inviting.

### Why this project

I chose this project because baking is one of my interests, and I want to understand more about why recipes work the way they do. Recipe websites do not always explain what will happen if something changes, while food-science articles usually explain one ingredient or technique without connecting it to an interactive recipe. This project has enough depth for five weeks because I will need to research baking science, model how changes affect each other, and present that information in a way that is easy to understand.

### Users

The main audience is home bakers who have some experience but want to learn more about how ingredients interact. They may be comfortable following a recipe or making a small change, but not understand why a batch spread, became dry, did not brown, or had an unexpected texture. The site should explain these changes without requiring advanced chemistry. More experienced bakers could use the ratio controls to develop variations, but the site will not replace real test bakes.

### Experience goals

I want users to feel curious and more confident about experimenting. The base recipe should be ready as soon as they enter the experiment. A simple texture control will let them start by moving the cookie toward cakier or crispier and seeing the connected ratio changes. They can then learn what each ingredient does, try a substitution or adjust a ratio, and compare their version with the original. Ratios will appear by default so users can focus on how ingredients relate. After choosing how many cookies they want, they can see the measurements for that yield. By the end, they should understand what may change and why.

### Technical goals

The website will keep track of the original and modified recipes. The texture control will update a researched group of ratios together, while the individual controls will let users make more specific changes. Either type of change should update the predicted qualities and explain what caused them. The selected yield will scale the ratios into measurements without changing the formula. Each supported change should connect to research, and the site should warn users when an experiment goes beyond what it can reasonably predict. It should work on desktop and mobile, and the texture control must also work with a keyboard and provide clear text labels instead of relying only on the slider's position.

### Starting point

I will build the project from scratch using this repository template. I will keep track of any research, code libraries, fonts, photographs, icons, or other outside material in `research.md`, including their sources and usage terms.

### Tech stack

I will use HTML, CSS, and JavaScript to build the website, with Vite providing the local development server and preparing the finished files for publishing. This should be enough to create the interactive recipe system without adding accounts, a database, or a server that the first version does not need. The recipe information and prediction rules can be stored as structured JavaScript data, which will make them easier to trace and revise. The finished site can be published through GitHub Pages or another static hosting service. I will only add a larger framework if the early prototype shows that the interaction is too difficult to manage without one.

### MVP or critical function

The MVP will focus on one base chocolate-chip cookie recipe that loads automatically and a limited group of common ingredients. Users will first be able to move a texture control toward cakier or crispier and see the related ratios change together. They will also be able to choose a supported substitution or adjust an individual ingredient ratio within a researched range. The site will then show the likely effects on the cookie, explain the most important changes, and warn the user if the experiment goes outside the model's limits.

Ratios will appear first, and users will select a desired number of cookies to generate the corresponding ingredient measurements. The most important function to test during Phase 2 is whether the rules can turn recipe changes into consistent and understandable predictions. I will test prepared examples, confirm that undoing a change returns to the original result, and make sure unsupported inputs produce a warning instead of a made-up prediction.

### Five-week map

**Phase 1 — Ideate and pitch:** Finalize the concept, audience, scope, brand, MVP, and risks, and get approval before development begins.

**Phase 2 — Prove the core:** Research the first recipe variables and build a basic version of the prediction system. It should support at least one substitution and one ratio adjustment from beginning to end.

**Phase 3 — Expand and test:** Add the approved ingredients, comparison views, supported ranges, and the first version of the visual style. Test combinations of rules and the main user flow.

**Phase 4 — Revise and prepare for peer review:** Improve the responsive design, accessibility, explanations, and error messages. Conduct usability testing, simplify anything confusing, and publish a stable version for peer review.

**Phase 5 — Finish and showcase:** Respond to feedback, verify the recipe model and interface, finish the branding and documentation, and publish the final site.

### Stretch goals

1. Compare ingredient temperatures and states, such as cold, softened, browned, or melted butter.
2. Compare techniques such as creaming time, mixing method, or chilled versus unchilled dough.
3. Let users save or export a modified recipe.
4. Keep a side-by-side history of experiments.
5. Include photographs from real test batches.
6. Add another cookie style or type of baked good with its own researched model.

### Risks

The biggest risk is making the baking predictions sound more certain than they really are. I will address this by limiting the model to changes I have researched, citing my sources, using language such as “likely,” and warning users when they go outside the supported ranges. I will test the prediction rules early before adding more ingredients.

Another risk is trying to include too much. Substitutions, adjustable ratios, techniques, custom recipes, and multiple types of baked goods would be too much to complete at the same level in five weeks. The MVP will stay focused on cookies, supported substitutions, and ratio controls. Techniques and additional recipes will remain stretch goals.

The interface could also become crowded if it shows every ratio, measurement, and predicted quality at the same time. To avoid this, it will show ratios first and then calculate practical measurements based on the user's selected yield. Early user testing will show whether this is easy to follow. Multiple ingredient changes may also create conflicting predictions, so the site will need clear rules and explanations instead of hiding everything behind one final score.
